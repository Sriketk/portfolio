Design is one of my biggest challenges in building software, across both front-end and back-end. Lacking a strong foundation in design, I often couldn't even tell when something was poorly designed. But once you interact with a system long enough, you discover that a product's performance is deeply tied to its design.

 Whether it was a front-end interface where I struggled to find the settings page or a back-end module that demanded code changes in ten different files for every minor update, design flaws were everywhere. The tricky part is that, while you can often sense if something is good or bad, it's much harder to pinpoint exactly why. Design is the sum of countless small details: the text-to-width ratio, icon alignment, file structures, and naming conventions all matter. I found that for the backend, I spent more time testing various API shapes, file structures, and names than actually writing code. Likewise, on the front end, I would spend hours examining buttons and cards just to understand what makes a component feel "right." The process is largely grunt work—one that can't be handed off to AI or easily parallelized. I had to repeat this painstaking routine for every design element: icons, fonts, and colors. My lowest GitHub commit weeks were those spent on design. Unlike engineering, where you can sometimes hack things together to reach a solution, design offers no shortcuts. It's either good or garbage.
