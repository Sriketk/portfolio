Sriket's working theory of how software work compounds — or fails to. The claim: for him, software requires consecutive hours of focused time to make progress, and the cost of context-switching out of a problem and back in is non-linear.

## The claim

"Software is something that, for me, I have to spend consecutive hours in order to make progress. It's so momentum-based. Losing your train of thought and picking it up later is just so dangerous and slows everything down."

The model: progress on a hard software problem requires dialing in until you really understand it. Once you've dialed in, the marginal next hour is enormously productive. Once you've broken the dial-in by switching contexts, recovering takes more than just the time of the interruption — it takes the time to re-load all the state you were holding.

## Where it originated

The belief crystallized at [[ally-financial-era]] under the meeting load. A typical workday gave him 3–4 meetings consuming roughly 3 hours, leaving 4 productive hours fragmented across the day rather than concentrated. The math of the day made it impossible to get into the deep dial-in state where real progress lives. He eventually started taking days off specifically to skip meetings and get consecutive work hours back.

## The corollary

Meetings are not just time-expensive — they're momentum-expensive. The expected value of an N-minute meeting is much more than N minutes of forgone work because it interrupts the consecutive-hours condition. This is why Sriket hated stand-up specifically: it took the most productive morning time and converted it into low-information status updates.

## Compatibility with other beliefs

Closely tied to [[design-cant-be-faked]] — both say that certain valuable work cannot be parallelized or fragmented, and that the practitioner has to defend uninterrupted attention. Anti-correlated with most corporate work patterns.
