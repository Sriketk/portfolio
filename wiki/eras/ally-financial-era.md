Sriket's first job out of college: a software engineer at Ally Financial back in Charlotte, North Carolina. The era starts in lostness, turns into a self-driven Next.js obsession, becomes a stint on the AI labs team, and ends in a slow-building decision to quit (see [[quit-ally]]).

## The first weeks: lost

Sriket entered Ally weak in computer science. He knew "a little bit of JavaScript, HTML, CSS, and how to solve medium LeetCode questions." He couldn't build anything. He didn't know AWS. His first day surfaced jargon he had no anchor for — CMS, Contentful — and the lostness kept compounding for weeks: Akamai, CDNs, containers, clusters. Even the work itself touched things he'd never heard of: Next.js, SSR, CSR, static site generation, incremental static regeneration.

He hated the feeling of not being able to contribute, of being lost in conversations and meetings. The honest version of his condition: helpless.

## The catch-up grind

Ally was a legacy bank — the company didn't demand long hours. Sriket imposed them on himself anyway. Ten- to eleven-hour days, in-office five days a week despite a three-day requirement, weekends in the office too. The pressure was internal: he had to learn everything and stop feeling lost.

This is when he first got serious about React. Critically, this was pre-Sonnet-3.5 — AI assistance was not yet really a thing. He found himself loving Next.js: he loved reading the docs, he found the design beautiful, the modern black-and-white finish read as "the future" to him. He became obsessed with finding the optimal way to fetch and render data. The obsession let him catch up fast — he was promoted half a year in.

## The AI labs team

He was reassigned to a labs-focused team building AI agents in LangGraph and LangChain in Python. From the website team he had previously looked up at this team — "Wow this team is probably the coolest stuff done with AI." Getting moved there felt like accomplishment.

The work itself: graph-based nodes, agents, conditional rendering, rendering UI of the agent graphs. In retrospect he calls it "hacky" — virtualenv pain, dependency hell, a single mismatched version breaking the chain. Python dependency management he flatly labels bad.

## Tech Twitter discovery

This is when he first started going on technology Twitter, and it hit him like a different planet. New vocabulary everywhere — he remembers "MCP" appearing constantly on his timeline and being lost, recognizing the exact same feeling from his early Ally weeks: everyone speaking a language he didn't have.

What he saw on Twitter — AI companies forming, evals proliferating — felt enormous and exciting. Meanwhile at work the trajectory was inverting: the more he excelled and learned, the more mundane the work became. He used to look up to teammates who he thought were doing crazy work; he reached a level where they could no longer answer his questions. When he asked why something was the way it was, the answer was often "it just is." What bothered him most was that they didn't care about learning more. He doesn't blame them — Ally was a corporate company where many people were there for job security — but it made him feel he had to drive his own learning. He blames himself, in part, for not having taken college more seriously, which is why he was in this position to begin with.

## The slow turn

Around nine months in, people started getting assigned to him: onboarding, interviewing, distributing work. He hated it. He felt this was his prime — he needed to learn as much as possible, not start teaching. He kept taking maximum work, hitting twelve-hour days and weekends.

Meetings drained him. He hated leading stand-up especially — forty minutes of the most productive morning time spent on updates that didn't matter, occasionally followed by his boss quizzing him on what X person or Y person did. He has a recurring framing of work: 3–4 meetings a day eats 3 hours from a 7-hour productive ceiling, and software requires consecutive hours because it's momentum-based (see [[software-momentum-based]]). He started taking days off specifically to skip meetings and actually work.

## The pull elsewhere

Tech Twitter showed him a world of ambitious startups. Two beliefs locked him in place at this stage: he wasn't good enough to land at a real startup (he kept seeing Waterloo kids with four big-tech internships filling those roles), and he was running out of energy to learn outside of work. He started sleeping less. The math of "four hours of learning outside work for six months to catch up" felt unbearable.

The decision to quit took shape — see [[quit-ally]].
